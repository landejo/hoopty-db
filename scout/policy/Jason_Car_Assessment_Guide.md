# Guide to How Jason Assesses Cars

## 1. How to use this guide

This is a working decision model, not a static list of favorite cars. Use it to analyze vehicles Jason saves, compare actual listings, assign risk-aware scores, identify missing evidence, recommend a maximum price, and suggest the next concrete action.

The central principle is:

> Jason wants a car with personality, but he does not want personality to become an excuse for unbounded repair risk, weak documentation, or an irrational price.

Always distinguish among:

1. **Listing facts:** What the advertisement, VIN history, receipts, photographs, videos, and seller actually establish.
2. **Seller claims:** Assertions that remain unverified, such as “fully serviced,” “rear welds intact,” or “doesn’t burn oil.”
3. **Inference:** A reasoned conclusion based on the evidence, labeled as inference.
4. **Recommendation:** What Jason should do, at what price, and subject to which conditions.

Preferences have changed as Jason’s circumstances have changed. Favor the newest stated strategy over older shopping briefs. Do not erase older preferences, because they still explain his tastes and may become relevant again when his finances are more flexible.

When the 335i was operating reliably, Jason could hold out for a car that met a higher enthusiast or “specialness” threshold. Now a lower-cost, reliable, readily available bridge car deserves stronger consideration even if it would have ranked lower in that earlier situation.
- The RX means Jason does **not** need to make a panicked same-day auction purchase. Temporary reliance on the RX is preferable to buying the wrong vehicle under artificial urgency. It creates time for inspection and price discipline, not an indefinite reason to postpone replacing the BMW.

### Financial strategy

- Earlier searches considered roughly $25,000–$30,000 enthusiast cars and similarly priced SUVs.
- The current strategy is different: preserve cash, prioritize debt reduction, and buy a lower-cost car that does not delay future financial flexibility.
- Jason expects that in roughly **6–24 months** he may be able to be much more selective and buy a better long-term enthusiast car.

- Current desired purchase price is **ideally $10,000–$13,000 and generally under $15,000**.
- “Under $15,000” should not mean a $14,900 purchase that immediately needs $5,000 of work. Use risk-adjusted, all-in cost.
- A somewhat more expensive car can occasionally make sense if it is unusually well documented, needs little immediate work, has low depreciation risk, and could serve as a keeper. Treat this as an exception that requires an explicit explanation.

### Current urgency mode: accelerated, not emergency

Represent the current state as `accelerated_bridge`, not `emergency` and not `casual_search`.

- **Why it is accelerated:** The 335i is no longer a dependable baseline. Recurring cooling trouble and known suspension needs increase the chance of more downtime or another repair decision.
- **Why it is not an emergency:** The recently purchased RX 350 supplies dependable household transportation, so Jason can still require records, inspection access, California compatibility, and a rational price.
- **How rankings should change:** Give more weight to near-term availability, reliability, local inspectability, low immediate needs, service support, and easy resale. Give somewhat less weight to rarity, perfect color, ultimate performance, and collector specification.
- **How the candidate pool should change:** Surface credible lower-cost bridge solutions that Jason might not have considered strongly while the 335i was healthy. Label them `pragmatic_bridge` rather than pretending they are dream cars.
- **What does not change:** Do not loosen safety, title, structural, emissions, catastrophic-risk, or total-cost standards. Do not revive Jason’s explicit exclusions merely because the BMW is leaking.

### Current mission

The replacement is Jason’s car, not the household’s sole utility vehicle. The RX already covers most practical needs. Therefore:

- A two-seat roadster or small coupe is viable.
- A compact hatchback is attractive because it combines fun with useful everyday space.
- Seating for five, maximum cargo space, and adventure-SUV capability are no longer mandatory for this particular purchase.
- Some practicality remains useful, but it should not overwhelm the desire for character.
- The intended experience includes regular driving, Carmel-area roads, weekend drives, and curvy-road enjoyment.
- Jason owned a **1997 BMW Z3 2.8** and already understands the Z3’s size, ergonomics, and fit. Do not reject a Z3 on generic “small roadster” comfort assumptions.

### Support and logistics

- California cars, especially Northern/Central California cars, are strongly preferred because they are easier to see, inspect, smog-check, and retrieve.
- West Coast cars are preferable to East Coast cars when the vehicles are otherwise similar.
- A distant car must be meaningfully better or cheaper after buyer fees, transport, travel, tax, immediate maintenance, and risk.
- Jason values an independent pre-purchase inspection. A local, inspectable car can be better than an apparently superior remote auction car.
- Jason’s mechanic, Mark Aiello, works on Volkswagens and recommends Toyota/Lexus reliability. He will not work on Land Rovers. Local serviceability therefore affects fit.
- California emissions compliance is a real gate. Do not assume a modified car will pass merely because it has passed elsewhere or because a seller says the changes are minor.

## 3. The two-car-strategy distinction

Every recommendation should identify which of these missions the car serves.

### Mission A: Current bridge car

The bridge car should be:

- Purchase price preferably $10,000–$13,000 and normally below $15,000.
- Interesting, quirky, or engaging enough that Jason does not resent buying it.
- Reliable enough for regular use.
- Free of a plausible catastrophic failure that would defeat the financial purpose of buying cheaply.
- Easy to inspect and service.
- Liquid enough to resell in 6–24 months without a punishing loss.
- Stock or close to stock, with documented maintenance.
- Available soon enough to replace the 335i without requiring a prolonged nationwide search.

In the current `accelerated_bridge` mode, a car can merit serious consideration with less emotional or collector appeal than Jason would normally require, provided it is still personally tolerable or mildly interesting and wins decisively on price, reliability, condition, and reversibility. The analysis must say when that is the reason it ranks well.

### Mission B: Future selective keeper

The future car can emphasize:

- A special specification, rare color combination, unusually low mileage, or collector interest.
- BMW Z3/M Coupe character, a particularly good M Roadster, or a properly inspected Porsche.
- Higher price if the car is genuinely superior rather than merely scarce.
- A more demanding nationwide search and patience for the right example.

Do not quietly score a Mission B car as though it were a sensible Mission A purchase. If a listing is attractive only because it could be a keeper, say that plainly.

## 4. Durable preferences

These have remained consistent across the searches.

### Driving and character

- A **manual transmission is strongly preferred and usually required for enthusiast cars**.
- Jason values steering feel, chassis balance, naturally aspirated response, compact dimensions, and analog character.
- He likes unusual silhouettes, uncommon specifications, cult cars, and vehicles with a clear identity.
- “Quirky” is positive when the car is mechanically coherent and usable. Quirky does not excuse poor design, poor reliability, or weak parts/service support.
- He responds to cars such as the Z3 Coupe/M Coupe “clown shoe,” Z3/M Roadster, MINI Cooper S, GTI, 128i, and Cayman because they feel distinct rather than generic.
- Color and interior combinations matter. Rare colors or extended-leather interiors can justify preference among otherwise comparable cars.
- A special color must never override structural, mechanical, title, or documentation problems.

### Condition and history

- Stock or mostly stock is preferred.
- Tasteful, cohesive, name-brand modifications can be acceptable.
- Cheap, incoherent, poorly documented, emissions-problematic, or abuse-signaling modifications are negative.
- Strong service records are more valuable than unusually low mileage with no age-related maintenance.
- Clean title is the strong default.
- Original paint is a positive, but honest quality paintwork is not automatically disqualifying.
- Matching, reputable tires matter. Mismatched or very cheap tires are a signal about ownership standards as well as a consumable expense.
- Warning lights, fluid leaks, cooling problems, unexplained noises, or seller evasiveness are substantial negatives.
- A cooperative seller who supplies VIN, receipts, underside photographs, cold-start video, scan data, and PPI access meaningfully improves a car’s standing.

### Reliability and risk

- Jason can accept ordinary old-car maintenance.
- He is trying to avoid low-frequency, very-high-cost failures and repeated diagnostic drama.
- Known model risks should be handled asymmetrically. A missing $500 maintenance record is not equivalent to missing evidence about a $20,000 engine risk.
- “It runs great” and “everything works” are weak evidence.
- Recent expensive service adds value only when documented and competently performed. It does not erase unrelated risks.

### Value

- Jason is price disciplined. Emotional appeal can influence which car to pursue, not eliminate the maximum price.
- The correct comparison is **risk-adjusted total acquisition cost**, not asking price or current auction bid.
- Prior sale and listing history for the same VIN matter. Relists, dealer flips, auction failures, and large markups should be identified.
- Jason will pay somewhat more for documented condition, unusually good specification, geographic convenience, and low immediate needs.
- He does not want to fund a dealer’s markup merely because a car moved from one venue to another.

### How to resolve Jason’s recurring preference tensions

Jason’s search is not simply “reliability first” or “fun first.” The tracker should preserve these tensions instead of flattening them into one generic buyer profile:

- **Reliability versus character:** Reliability is a threshold, not the sole objective. A generic but dependable car can still be a poor fit. The ideal bridge car is dependable *and* has a point of view.
- **German-car familiarity versus German-car fatigue:** Jason understands and enjoys older BMWs, especially the Z3, but the 335i has made him wary of turbo complexity, cooling failures, and serial repair exposure. A naturally aspirated 128i or Z3 can fit; another cheap turbo BMW usually does not.
- **Low mileage versus age:** Low mileage is attractive but never substitutes for age-related service. A 25-year-old 29k-mile car can need more immediate recommissioning than a consistently driven 80k-mile car with excellent records.
- **Rarity versus condition:** Rare colors, coupes, extended leather, hardtops, and unusual specifications matter. They break ties and can support some premium, but they do not offset structure, engine, title, emissions, or documentation problems.
- **Cheap acquisition versus cheap ownership:** A low bid, salvage title, or deferred-maintenance car may be inexpensive to acquire and expensive to own. Score the latter.
- **Local ordinary car versus remote special car:** Proximity, inspection access, and California compliance can outweigh a better-looking specification thousands of miles away.
- **Bridge car versus keeper:** Low depreciation does not automatically make a $25k keeper compatible with a $10k–$15k cash-preservation strategy. If recommending the exception, explicitly show why it is preferable to waiting.
- **Toyota/Lexus rationality versus duplication:** Jason appreciates Toyota/Lexus reliability, but the RX already supplies it. A second Lexus must deliver a distinct capability or personality rather than merely repeating the same role.

When preferences conflict, resolve them in this order:

1. Safety, legal/California-registration feasibility, and catastrophic-risk gates.
2. Current mission and budget.
3. Quality and completeness of evidence.
4. Risk-adjusted all-in value.
5. Driving character and specification.
6. Rarity, color, and collector appeal.

## 7. Evidence hierarchy

Score evidence by quality, not merely by the number of claims.

### Strong evidence

- Itemized invoices identifying date, mileage, parts, and shop.
- Clear photographs of the actual area at risk.
- Specialist PPI report.
- Diagnostic scan or model-specific test data.
- VIN-linked history from reputable sources.
- Cold-start and driving videos that address a specific question.
- Before/after photographs and invoices for structural or collision repair.

### Useful but incomplete evidence

- Carfax/AutoCheck service entries without itemized invoices.
- Owner-maintained logs supported by parts receipts.
- Long ownership combined with coherent records.
- Seller photographs that are relevant but incomplete.

### Weak evidence

- “Fully serviced.”
- “No issues.”
- “Mechanic checked it.”
- “Rear welds intact.”
- “Does not consume oil.”
- “No smoke on startup.”
- “Minor damage only.”
- “Southern car” or “California car” without underside evidence.

### Unknown is not good

Do not translate missing information into a neutral or positive assumption. Mark it `unknown`, reduce documentation/confidence scores, state what would resolve it, and reflect the uncertainty in the maximum price.

## 8. Gates before scoring

A numeric score must not allow attractive color, low mileage, or a cheap bid to compensate for a model-critical unknown.

### Hard reject gates

Ordinarily use `Reject` or `Do not pursue` when any of these applies:

- Seller refuses to provide a VIN or allow a reasonable independent inspection.
- Clear title fraud, odometer inconsistency, or materially contradictory identity/specification remains unresolved.
- Active overheating, unexplained coolant loss, major oil-pressure concern, serious brake issue, or unsafe structural problem.
- Heavy structural rust or collision repair of unknown quality.
- California registration or emissions appears infeasible.
- A model-specific catastrophic-risk requirement is missing and cannot be completed before a binding purchase. The current Cayman S borescope rule is the clearest example.
- Total expected cost defeats the purpose of the current bridge strategy.

### Conditional gates

These usually cap the verdict at `Maybe / verify` until resolved:

- Salvage/rebuilt title.
- Meaningful accident history without repair records and measurements.
- Missing Z3/M Roadster trunk-floor/differential-mount evidence.
- Missing GX470 timing-belt/water-pump evidence.
- Non-stock engine software or powertrain modifications without supporting records and California-smog proof.
- Permanent warning lights.
- Remote auction with no opportunity for a PPI.
- Major service claimed but not documented.

### Manual-transmission gate

- For a saved listing that is meant to satisfy the fun-car brief, an automatic is ordinarily `Reject: wrong configuration` even if the car itself is good.
- For a listing Jason has saved specifically as a low-cost `pragmatic_bridge`, an automatic is not automatically disqualified. It must compensate with unusually strong reliability, price, condition, convenience, and resale logic, and the assessment must state that it is solving the immediate bridge problem rather than the enthusiast brief.
- Do not apply this gate to GX/Lexus SUV searches or another explicitly automatic vehicle class.

## 9. Recommended scoring model

Use gates first. Then score the vehicle out of 100.

| Category | Points | What it measures |
|---|---:|---|
| Documentation and verifiability | 30 | VIN clarity, ownership history, receipts, relevant photos, diagnostic evidence, seller cooperation, PPI access |
| Mechanical, structural, and cosmetic condition | 25 | Current condition, leaks, cooling, suspension, structure, tires, warning lights, deferred work |
| Price and risk-adjusted value | 15 | Asking/hammer price, comps, previous VIN history, fees, transport, immediate work, downside/resale |
| Mission fit | 15 | Bridge versus keeper fit, current urgency mode, manual, reliability, near-term availability, regular usability, practicality, Carmel-road enjoyment |
| Logistics and inspectability | 10 | Distance, California registration/smog, ability to inspect, transport complexity, local serviceability |
| Emotional/specification fit | 5 | Character, color, rarity, body style, special options, subjective desirability |
| **Total** | **100** | |

### Suggested interpretation

- **85–100: Pursue.** Strong evidence, good fit, and defensible price. Still require the appropriate PPI.
- **75–84: Pursue conditionally.** Worth acting on, with named conditions.
- **60–74: Maybe / verify.** Interesting but material unknowns or price mismatch remain.
- **Below 60: Reject.** Poor fit, excessive risk, inadequate value, or too many unknowns.

### Score caps

- Active hard-reject gate: verdict is `Reject` regardless of arithmetic score.
- Salvage/rebuilt title without full repair evidence and a specialist PPI: maximum verdict `Maybe / verify`.
- Missing model-critical evidence: cap documentation points and verdict as specified in the model section.
- Automatic transmission in a manual-required search: `Reject: wrong configuration` rather than a misleading low numeric score.

### Confidence score

Maintain a separate `confidence` score from 0–100. This describes confidence in the assessment, not quality of the car.

- 80–100: Extensive relevant records, photos, history, and inspection evidence.
- 50–79: Enough information for a provisional recommendation.
- Below 50: Listing is too thin; verdict should rarely exceed `Maybe / verify`.

## 10. Price discipline

Always show at least these numbers:

- Asking price or current bid.
- Buyer fee.
- Estimated transport/travel.
- Immediate known maintenance.
- Allowance for overdue age/mileage service.
- Model-specific risk reserve.
- Estimated total acquisition cost before and, when possible, after California tax/registration.
- Recommended offer range.
- Maximum hammer price or walk-away price.

For an auction:

- Do not treat an early current bid as the vehicle’s probable sale price.
- Identify whether the auction has a reserve.
- Account for the platform’s buyer fee.
- Note the exact closing time in Pacific Time.
- Avoid a last-minute bid when critical evidence cannot be obtained before bids become binding.
- Search the VIN for prior auctions, dealer listings, price reductions, and failed sales.

The maximum bid should be calculated backward from an acceptable all-in cost:

`maximum hammer = acceptable all-in cost - buyer fee - transport/travel - immediate service - risk reserve - tax/registration allowance`

Do not create false precision when a major input is unknown. Use a range and explain the swing factor.

## 11. Universal listing-analysis workflow

### Step 1: Normalize the listing

Extract and verify:

- VIN.
- Year, make, model, trim, engine, and body style.
- Manual/automatic transmission.
- Mileage.
- Exterior/interior colors.
- Asking price or live bid.
- Dealer/private seller.
- Location and title state.
- Clean, salvage, rebuilt, lemon, or unknown title.
- Ownership count and duration of current ownership.
- Accident/damage entries.
- Factory options.
- Modifications.
- Included records, keys, manuals, accessories, and spare parts.
- Known flaws and warning lights.
- Auction end time, reserve status, fee, and comments.

Flag contradictions. For example, a Facebook listing titled “2000 Z3 3.0i Coupe” described the engine as a 2.8. The tracker should decode the VIN and identify that discrepancy instead of accepting the headline.

### Step 2: Search the VIN

Look for:

- Previous auction results.
- Previous dealer asking prices.
- Changes in mileage.
- Changes in condition narrative.
- Added or removed modifications.
- Previously disclosed damage that disappears from a later advertisement.
- Dealer flips and markup percentage.

### Step 3: Apply model-specific gates

Examples:

- Cayman S: borescope and related engine-risk evidence.
- Z3/M Roadster/M Coupe: trunk-floor spot welds and differential mount.
- GX470: timing belt, water pump, suspension, rust, cooling, and warning lights.
- Tuned GTI/MINI/BMW: clutch, emissions, cooling, and abuse implications.

### Step 4: Estimate catch-up work

Low mileage does not cancel age. Consider:

- Tires by date code.
- Cooling-system plastics, hoses, radiator, pump, and thermostat.
- Rubber bushings and mounts.
- Fluids.
- Brakes.
- Battery.
- Convertible top and hydraulics.
- A/C.
- Leaks and gaskets.

### Step 5: Score, state confidence, and give an action

Every assessment should end with:

- Verdict.
- Score and confidence.
- Why it fits or conflicts with Jason’s current mission.
- Three most important positives.
- Three most important concerns.
- Missing evidence.
- Questions for the seller.
- PPI focus.
- Recommended offer and maximum price.
- Exact next action.

## 12. Model-specific rules

### 12.1 BMW Z3 3.0 roadster and coupe

#### What Jason wants

- Primarily **2001–2002 3.0-liter cars with a manual transmission**.
- Sport Package is a meaningful positive.
- The 3.0 is preferred over the earlier 2.8 for its stronger performance and desirability.
- Manual is materially preferable to automatic. Automatic coupes should not be treated as substitutes for the desired manual car merely because coupes are rare.
- Roadster is the financially accessible choice.
- Coupe is more distinctive and emotionally attractive, but currently carries a substantial rarity premium.

#### Rear-structure rule

The Z3 uses a single-ear differential attachment tied into the trunk-floor structure. The concern is not accurately summarized as merely “the rear subframe.” Inspect:

- Visible trunk-floor spot welds, especially on the left side.
- Raised or separated welds.
- Cracked seam sealer.
- Rust halos around welds.
- Distortion of the floor.
- Differential ear and mount.
- Crossmember-to-floor/frame-rail areas underneath.

For a stock, street-driven **3.0 non-M**, a Randy Forbes-style reinforcement is **not mandatory** if a proper inspection shows a completely sound original structure. Lack of reinforcement should not automatically reject the car.

Reinforcement becomes more valuable when the car is:

- An M model.
- Lowered or fitted with stiff bushings.
- Modified for more power.
- Track-driven.
- Already showing failed welds or cracks.

A seller’s statement that “the welds are intact” is insufficient without photographs or a specialist inspection.

#### Other inspection priorities

- Cooling-system age and documentation.
- VANOS/CCV and oil-leak symptoms.
- Suspension bushings, shocks, mounts, and alignment.
- Tire age, not just tread.
- Seat bushings and seat movement.
- Convertible-top condition and plastic rear window on roadsters.
- Water ingress.
- A/C operation.
- Accident repair and paintwork.

#### Price lessons

- A 29k-mile, one-owner, stock 2001 Z3 3.0 manual roadster in Sterling Gray was advertised first at $19,995 and then $18,995. It had excellent narrative provenance, but Jason’s disciplined target was approximately $16,500–$17,500, subject to records and structural inspection.
- Manual 3.0 Coupes can sell well into the $20,000s. One relevant coupe sold around $26,000. That supports the market premium but does not make every coupe worth that amount.
- A rare-color car can rank highly emotionally while still receiving a lower maximum price for prior damage, corrosion, warning lights, or missing structure evidence.

### 12.2 BMW M Roadster and M Coupe

#### What Jason wants

- S52-powered M Roadster, ideally below roughly **60,000 miles**.
- S52 M Coupe, ideally below roughly **80,000 miles**.
- The M Coupe is a top emotional choice, but its current price premium generally makes it a future-car purchase.
- The S52 is attractive because it combines character and performance with a simpler ownership proposition than later, more expensive variants.
- Manual transmission is inherent and essential.
- Documented Randy Forbes or equivalent reinforcement is a strong positive, especially on M cars.

#### Inspection priorities

- Full Z3 rear-structure inspection as described above.
- Cooling system, regardless of mileage.
- Oil leaks, VANOS behavior, motor/transmission mounts.
- Clutch and gearbox operation.
- Suspension and rear shock mounts.
- Tire age and match.
- A/C operation.
- Soft-top condition; a good factory hardtop is a meaningful value enhancer.

#### Salvage-title rule

A cheap salvage M Roadster is not automatically a bargain. A local $8,800 salvage-title example with a claim that its rear welds were intact illustrated the problem: low price and proximity create inspection opportunity, but do not establish collision quality or structural soundness.

Require:

- Pre-loss and post-loss photographs.
- Repair invoices.
- Exact reason for salvage branding.
- Frame/unibody measurements if structurally relevant.
- Airbag and restraint-system confirmation.
- Evidence of California registration/smog feasibility.
- Specialist PPI covering both collision repair and Z3-specific rear structure.
- A major price discount that recognizes permanent resale impairment.

#### Auction lessons

- A cheap auction result is not proof that Jason missed a good car. A 2000 M Roadster sold around $10,900 after disclosures including inoperative turn signals, paint/top history, and other uncertainty. Jason appropriately declined to treat the low price as sufficient reason to buy.
- A 29k-mile 1999 M Roadster was attractive as a possible keeper, but a nonfunctional A/C system, no stated cooling-system history, no rear-floor evidence, remote location, fees, and transport made it poorly aligned with the under-$15k bridge mission.
- A 123k-mile red 2000 M Roadster showed a very low early auction bid, but the same VIN had been advertised by the dealer around $18,900. The low live bid was not a meaningful expected purchase price, and the reserve mattered.

A 2018 GTI SE manual is representative of the kind of car worth considering, but the actual listing must still win on history, condition, and price.

### 12.6 Porsche 987 Boxster and Cayman

These are mainly future-car candidates at current prices.

#### Preferred hierarchy developed in discussion

1. **2009–2012 987.2 Boxster 2.9 manual:** attractive because the 9A1 engine avoids the earlier IMS architecture and has very low bore-scoring concern. A good example was expected around $25,000–$30,000.
2. **2007–2008 Cayman 2.7 manual:** less power than an S, but substantially more comfortable from a bore-scoring-risk perspective. A good 40k–65k-mile car was expected roughly around $22,000–$26,000.
3. **2006–2008 Cayman S 3.4 manual:** most exciting, but buy only with unusually strong due diligence.

#### Cayman S non-negotiable evidence

For an M97 3.4-liter Cayman S:

- Require an independent Porsche-specialist borescope with retained photographs, especially of cylinders 4–6.
- Obtain a DME over-rev report.
- Compression and leakdown testing are desirable and should be interpreted with the borescope, not used as a substitute for it.
- Inspect cooling-system condition and maintenance.
- Investigate oil consumption, startup smoke, soot pattern, and cylinder-related noise—but do not treat their absence as proof of clean cylinder walls.
- Verify clutch/flywheel, AOS, water pump, and other major service.
- Verify California smog compliance with the exact installed modifications.

Current rule:

> **No satisfactory borescope, no Cayman S purchase.**

A 2006 Cayman S with 103,700 miles illustrated this clearly. It had good options and meaningful prior service, but the seller confirmed there was no borescope and no DME over-rev report. The seller’s observation that it did not use oil or smoke was not equivalent evidence. Verdict: `Reject`, even at an attractive bid.

### 12.7 Lexus GX470/GX460 and other SUVs

The GX remains relevant only as a separate capability-oriented branch. It is not a manual fun-car substitute.

#### GX470 non-negotiable screening

- Documented timing-belt service.
- Documented water-pump service, ideally performed with the belt.
- Sorted suspension.
- No warning lights.
- No sagging rear air suspension.
- No heavy rust; light surface rust can be evaluated.
- Matching quality tires.
- No cheap lift.
- No unexplained coolant loss, oil leaks, brake issues, or radiator concerns.
- VIN, history, and PPI cooperation.

Tasteful Bilstein/Old Man Emu-type modifications can be acceptable, but permanent warning lights, questionable geometry, incomplete receipts, or corrosion still reduce value.

#### Current strategic limitation

Because the household already has the RX 350, a GX creates substantial functional overlap, uses much more fuel, and removes the fun-car slot. Recommend it only when Jason explicitly wants its old-school V8/body-on-frame character or off-road/towing capability.

#### Listing lessons

- A 2004 GX470 with 161k miles had a recent timing belt, water pump, thermostat, suspension, coil conversion, and tires. It was mechanically appealing, but prior rear damage, corrosion, a flashing suspension light, dealer-flip history, and an unanswered transmission-service question prevented the recent work from making it an automatic buy.
- That same VIN had sold for $12,250 earlier in 2026. VIN-level history was essential to understanding the later auction and likely reserve.
- A blue 2007 GX470 with 141k miles looked cleaner and more original, but the listing supplied no timing-belt/water-pump proof, retained the aging air/AVS system, used cheap tires, and had a cracked dashboard. Its low early bid was not evidence of value.

## 14. Reusable lessons from specific listings

These examples are historical calibration points, not necessarily active recommendations.

1. **Rare car, weak evidence:** A rare-color Z3 Coupe can be emotionally first but still require a lower maximum bid because structural photos, smog answers, or history are missing.
2. **Markup detection:** A Vancouver-area Z3 Coupe sold for approximately $22,337 and was soon relisted around $29,500 without a clearly documented transformation. The tracker should expose the delta and ask what value was added.
3. **Headline mismatch:** A Durham listing called a car a 2000 Z3 3.0i Coupe while the seller narrative identified a 2.8. Decode the VIN and model year; do not score a mislabeled car as the desired trim.
4. **Low mileage is not full maintenance:** A 29k-mile Z3 may still need cooling-system, tire, rubber, fluid, A/C, and structural evaluation.
5. **Recent service is not absolution:** A GX can receive thousands of dollars of recent parts and still have collision, corrosion, transmission-history, warning-light, or modification-quality concerns.
6. **Cheap auction bid is not a price:** Early bids on reserve auctions—particularly dealer cars—say little about the likely sale or reserve.
7. **Seller symptom reports are not diagnostic tests:** No oil use or smoke does not replace a Cayman S borescope.
8. **Reinforcement nuance:** Lack of a Randy Forbes kit is not fatal for a clean, stock Z3 3.0. Lack of a proper inspection remains a problem.
9. **Salvage is not automatically value:** A local $8,800 M Roadster can still be a poor bridge car if the collision history and structure are unknown.
10. **Best opportunity versus best car:** A time-sensitive auction may be the best listing to investigate today without being the car Jason would most confidently own.

## 17. AI behavior rules for the tracker

1. **Refresh volatile facts.** Prices, bids, auction status, mileage, and availability go stale quickly.
2. **Preserve the evidence source.** Attach each important claim to listing text, photo, receipt, history report, seller comment, or external VIN result.
3. **Never manufacture certainty.** If photos cannot be inspected, say so.
4. **Do not equate clean Carfax with accident-free reality.** It means no reported event in that source.
5. **Do not reward verbosity.** A long seller description can contain less evidence than one invoice.
6. **Surface contradictions automatically.** Year/engine/trim, mileage, title state, ownership, service dates, and accident descriptions should reconcile.
7. **Separate condition from documentation.** The car may be good while the listing evidence is weak; score and confidence should show the difference.
8. **Apply asymmetric risk.** Missing Cayman borescope evidence matters more than a missing second key.
9. **Calculate the real price.** Fees, shipping, immediate service, smog, tires, title penalty, and risk reserve belong in the recommendation.
10. **Respect the present strategy.** A wonderful $28,000 car may be a good future target and a poor current recommendation.
11. **Model urgency correctly.** The unreliable 335i increases the value of a cheap, reliable, immediately available car; the RX prevents that urgency from justifying a higher ceiling, waived inspection, or excessive risk.
12. **Broaden without becoming generic.** Surface strong pragmatic bridge candidates Jason might previously have overlooked, but explain why they fit now and do not reintroduce explicit exclusions.
13. **State what would change the verdict.** A useful assessment tells Jason how a car could move from `Maybe` to `Pursue`.
